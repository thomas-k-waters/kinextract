The forward model, term by term

A complete account of how a stellar template library, a line-of-sight velocity distribution, and a continuum correction combine into a predicted spectrum — and how that prediction is fit to data. Written for reference, not for a first read.

2The data-generating (forward) model

This is the single equation that says: given a population mixture, a velocity distribution, and a continuum shape, here is the spectrum we'd observe.

Eq. 1 — forward model
f(λi)=C(λi)⋅∑k=1Kwk∑j=1Jbj⋅Tk(λi1+vj/c)f(\lambda_i) = C(\lambda_i) \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \sum_{j=1}^{J} b_j \cdot T_k\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{1+v_j/c}\right)
  • Tk(λ)T_k(\lambda)
    Template k's rest-frame flux — fixed input data, never fitted.
  • Tk(λi1+vj/c)T_k\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{1+v_j/c}\right)
    That template Doppler-shifted by velocity vj and linearly interpolated onto the observed grid. A first-order Doppler approximation, no relativistic correction.
  • ∑jbj⋅Tk(…)\sum_j b_j \cdot T_k(\dots)
    The Doppler convolution of template k with the LOSVD — a Riemann-sum discretization of a continuous integral (see §7).
  • wk≥0w_k \geq 0
    How much of the observed light is template k — a population-mixture fraction, up to overall scale (§8).
  • C(λi)C(\lambda_i)
    A single shared multiplicative continuum correction, applied after the population mixture is built.
Convolution notation

Convolution is an operation between two functions; the sum over j above is that operation in discretized form. The compact way to write the same equation:

Eq. 2
(Tk*b)(λi)=∑j=1Jbj⋅Tk(λi1+vj/c)(T_k * b)(\lambda_i) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} b_j \cdot T_k\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{1+v_j/c}\right)
Eq. 3 — compact forward model
f(λi)=C(λi)⋅∑k=1Kwk(Tk*b)(λi)f(\lambda_i) = C(\lambda_i) \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \, (T_k * b)(\lambda_i)

3How the LOSVD and continuum are parameterized

Neither b nor C is a free vector of numbers — both are forced positive by construction, via an exponential link over a B-spline:

Eq. 4 — LOSVD
b(v)=exp⁡(∑m=1McmBm(v))b(v) = \exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M} c_m B_m(v)\right)
Eq. 5 — continuum
C(λ)=exp⁡(∑n=1NcdnBnc(λ))C(\lambda) = \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N_c} d_n B_n^{c}(\lambda)\right)

B_m(v) and B_n^c(λ) are cubic B-spline basis functions — B_m on the velocity grid (knot spacing auto-scaled to stay near 95 km/s regardless of target σ), B_n^c on the wavelength grid (deliberately coarser — a handful of knots, since its job is absorbing smooth continuum shape, not carrying spectral information). The only free parameters being fit are the coefficient vectors c and d — the spline basis and the exponential link are fixed structure.

Why exponentiate?

exp(·) is positive everywhere in parameter space, so gradient-based optimization never has to deal with a boundary — there is no "wall" at zero to get stuck against, unlike enforcing b ≥ 0 directly as a constraint.

4The full objective being minimized

Eq. 6
ri=yi−f(λi)r_i = y_i - f(\lambda_i)
Eq. 7 — the objective
ℒ(w,c,d)=12r⊤Σ−1r+λLOSVD2∑m(D2c)m2+λc2∑n(D2d)n2+λwing2∑jτjbj2\mathcal{L}(w, c, d) = \frac{1}{2} r^\top \Sigma^{-1} r + \frac{\lambda_{\text{LOSVD}}}{2} \sum_{m} \left(D_2 c\right)_m^2 + \frac{\lambda_c}{2} \sum_{n} \left(D_2 d\right)_n^2 + \frac{\lambda_{\text{wing}}}{2} \sum_{j} \tau_j b_j^2
The first term is an ordinary weighted χ² once correlated noise is accounted for (§5); the rest is a sum of three independent penalty terms. Every symbol, defined precisely below.
  • r, Σ
    r is the residual vector from Eq. 6. Σ is the noise covariance (§5) — in practice this term is never computed this way directly; see §5 for why.
  • λLOSVD
    The LOSVD curvature-penalty weight — this is what earlier sections and the codebase call lam (default 2.0). Named explicitly here to distinguish it from λc below, since the two are independent knobs, not the same number reused.
  • (D₂c)m
    The second difference of the LOSVD spline coefficients (Eq. 8 below) — penalizes curvature of the coefficient sequence, not its raw size, scaled by the grid's real velocity spacing so a given λLOSVD means the same thing regardless of grid width.
  • c
    The LOSVD's own spline coefficients — literally the same c as Eq. 4 (b(v) = exp(ΣcmBm(v))), not a new unknown introduced here. m runs 1..M as in §1.
  • λc
    The continuum curvature-penalty weight — the codebase's lam_c (default 2.0). Independent of λLOSVD; only relevant when continuum-cofitting is on.
  • (D₂d)n
    The same kind of second-difference operator as for c, but acting on the continuum's own, separate coefficient vector on the wavelength grid — a different matrix (different size) from the one acting on c, even though both are called D₂.
  • d
    The continuum's own spline coefficients — literally the same d as Eq. 5 (C(λ) = exp(ΣdnBcn(λ))). n runs 1..Nc as in §1.
  • λwing
    Optional wing-amplitude penalty weight (lam_wing in code) — 0 (off) by default; largely superseded by the third-difference default's own wing behavior (see staleness notice above), and in any case not validated as safe across every S/N regime, so left as an explicit opt-in rather than a default.
  • bj
    The LOSVD's own value at velocity bin j — i.e. bj = b(vj), the same discretized b from Eq. 1. j runs 1..J as in §1.
  • τj
    The wing taper (Eq. 9 below) — 0 in the LOSVD's core, 1 in the far wings, so the λwing term only ever penalizes wing amplitude, never core amplitude.
  • wk ≥ 0
    The template weights from Eq. 1 enter as a hard constraint (solved via non-negative least squares), not as a penalty term in this objective.
Eq. 8 — the second-difference operator, explicitly
(D2c)m=cm+2−2cm+1+cm(D_2 c)_m = c_{m+2} - 2c_{m+1} + c_m
The ordinary discrete second derivative of the coefficient sequence (before the velocity/wavelength-spacing rescaling mentioned above) — large when three consecutive coefficients bend sharply, zero for a locally straight-line sequence. Penalizing the sum of these squared is the P-spline roughness penalty (Eilers & Marx 1996). The same formula applies to d, on its own (different-length) coefficient vector.
Eq. 9 — the wing taper
τj=11+exp⁡(−dj−onsetwidth),dj=|vj−vcenter|(vmax−vmin)/2\tau_j = \dfrac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{d_j - \text{onset}}{\text{width}}\right)}, \qquad d_j = \dfrac{|v_j - v_{\text{center}}|}{(v_{\max}-v_{\min})/2}
onset (default 0.45) and width (default 0.08) are fractions of the grid's own half-range, so the taper's shape scales automatically with the target's σ rather than needing per-target retuning. Under this package's standard grid convention (vmin/vmax = V ∓ 7σ), onset=0.45 works out to about 3.15σ from center — a smooth logistic ramp rather than a hard cutoff, for the same reason the moment-windowing taper in §9 is smooth: a hard cutoff is asymmetric for skewed densities and introduces its own bias.

5The noise model

Eq. 10 — AR(1) covariance
Σii′=σiσi′ρ|i−i′|\Sigma_{ii'} = \sigma_i \sigma_{i'} \rho^{|i-i'|}
σi — per-pixel noise standard deviation (from the error spectrum). ρ — a single correlation-strength parameter for pixel-to-pixel noise correlation. ρ = 0 recovers the default independent-pixel assumption; real MUSE data measures ρ ≈ 0.1–0.5.

Why Σ never appears explicitly. The statistically correct objective for correlated noise replaces the ordinary sum-of-squares with a weighted one, rᵀΣ⁻¹r (the Mahalanobis distance) — and computing that directly would mean inverting an N×N matrix every time the residual is evaluated, expensive and a real rewrite of every fitting function in the package. The fix is a change of variables, not a change of objective.

What a Cholesky factor is. Any valid covariance matrix Σ is symmetric and positive-definite, and every such matrix can be written as Σ = LLᵀ for a unique lower-triangular matrix L with positive diagonal entries — the Cholesky factorization. "Lower-triangular" is the property that makes it useful here: solving Lz = v for z is just forward substitution (one new unknown introduced per row), an O(N²) computation, versus O(N³) to invert Σ directly.

Eq. 11 — why whitening works
r⊤Σ−1r=r⊤(LL⊤)−1r=(L−1r)⊤(L−1r)=∥L−1r∥2=∥rw∥2r^\top \Sigma^{-1} r = r^\top (LL^\top)^{-1} r = (L^{-1}r)^\top (L^{-1}r) = \|L^{-1}r\|^2 = \|r_w\|^2
Substituting Σ = LLᵀ and regrouping shows the weighted sum-of-squares is exactly equal to an ordinary, unweighted sum-of-squares on the whitened residual r_w = L⁻¹r. That's the whole trick: whiten the inputs once (below), and every fitting function already in the package — which only ever computes a plain sum-of-squares — is automatically doing the statistically correct, covariance-weighted thing, with zero changes to any of them.
Eq. 12 — whitening
Σ=LL⊤,Aw=L−1A,yw=L−1y\Sigma = LL^\top, \qquad A_w = L^{-1}A, \qquad y_w = L^{-1}y
In code this is scipy.linalg.cholesky to get L, then solve_triangular (forward substitution) to apply L⁻¹ to A and y — a generic routine that works for any valid Σ, not special-cased to the AR(1) form of Eq. 10.
Making it concrete: what whitening actually does, pixel by pixel

For this package's specific AR(1) Σ, working out L⁻¹ by hand (rather than trusting the generic routine as a black box) gives a simple, two-term recursive filter — the same recursion an AR(1) process is generated by, run backwards:

Eq. 13 — the whitening filter, explicitly
yw,1=y1σ1,yw,i=yi/σi−ρ⋅yi−1/σi−11−ρ2(i≥2)y_{w,1} = \frac{y_1}{\sigma_1}, \qquad y_{w,i} = \frac{y_i/\sigma_i - \rho \cdot y_{i-1}/\sigma_{i-1}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}} \quad (i \geq 2)
(Same recursion applies independently to every column of A, and to y as shown.)

In words: normalize each pixel by its own noise level, then subtract off ρ times the previous normalized pixel, and rescale by 1/√(1−&rho²) to restore unit variance. This is exactly the inverse of the generative recipe for an AR(1) process itself (xi = ρxi−1 + √(1−&rho²)·zi, zi independent) — whitening un-does exactly the correlation that generated it. At ρ=0 the second term vanishes and this collapses to the plain yi/σi division used everywhere before this noise model existed — confirmed to match bit-for-bit, not just approximately. The code doesn't hard-code this formula (it calls the generic dense routine in Eq. 12, which works for any covariance, not just AR(1)), but since a Cholesky factorization is unique, that generic routine is provably computing exactly this recursion under the hood for this specific Σ — seeing it in closed form is what makes "whitening" concrete rather than a black box.

6How it's actually solved

(w, b, C) are never solved simultaneously — three-block alternating minimization: fix two, solve the third (in closed form or by local optimization), cycle until the joint objective stops improving.

  • step 1
    Fix (w, C), solve b — nonlinear (L-BFGS-B), multi-start pool since the exp-link makes this non-convex.
  • step 2
    Fix (b, C), solve w — non-negative least squares, exactly convex.
  • step 3
    Fix (b, w), solve C — nonlinear, small, its own multi-start pool.

Multi-start exists because a single starting point silently lands in a measurably-worse local minimum in roughly 1-in-150 fits.

The Doppler-convolution step is essentially free; the alternating multi-start optimization is the entire cost. This is why switching the forward model to log-wavelength + FFT convolution — the standard trick elsewhere in this field — would not meaningfully speed anything up here (see §9).

7What's outside the fitted equation entirely

LSF matching. Before a template ever enters Tk, it's convolved with a Gaussian to match the instrument's own wavelength-dependent line-spread function:

Eq. 14 — LSF matching
(w,b)∼(αw,b/α)for any α>0(w,\ b) \sim (\alpha w,\ b/\alpha) \quad \text{for any } \alpha > 0
A pre-processing transform on Tk, not a fitted parameter — you can only broaden a spectrum to match a coarser instrument, never sharpen it (hence max(0,·)). Known gap: assumes a Gaussian LSF shape; real instrument LSFs (MUSE included) are documented as measurably non-Gaussian, which can bias h₃/h₄ by up to ±0.1 — not yet corrected for.

Wavelength calibration / masking. Bad pixels, cosmic rays, and sky residuals are dropped before fitting, not weighted down within it.

8Explicit identifiability facts and gaps

Eq. 15 — scale non-identifiability
σmatch2(λ)=max⁡(0,σtarget2(λ)−σtemplate2(λ))\sigma_{\text{match}}^2(\lambda) = \max\left(0,\ \sigma_{\text{target}}^2(\lambda) - \sigma_{\text{template}}^2(\lambda)\right)
The overall scale of w and b is not separately determined by the data — only the shape of b and the relative mixture w/Σw are meaningful. Never compare raw w or b amplitudes across two different fits.
  • shared kinematics
    One LOSVD across all templates — not per-population kinematics (a grouped-LOSVD extension is a real, discussed possibility, not built).
  • shared continuum
    One continuum across all templates.
  • noise shape
    Σ's off-diagonal structure is a single scalar ρ (AR(1)), not a measured kernel.
  • no dust term
    No reddening term separate from whatever the continuum spline absorbs.

9Design notes

Why not log-wavelength?

Most spectral-fitting codes in this field (pPXF included) work in log-wavelength, because a Doppler shift is multiplicative in λ — a fixed fractional shift, Δλ/λ = v/c — which only becomes a fixed additive shift after taking a log:

Eq. 16 — continuous forward model
fk(λ)=∫b(v)Tk(λ1+v/c)dvf_k(\lambda) = \int b(v)\, T_k\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{1+v/c}\right) dv
Eq. 17 — log-wavelength substitution
ln⁡λobs=ln⁡λrest+ln⁡(1+v/c)\ln\lambda_{\text{obs}} = \ln\lambda_{\text{rest}} + \ln(1+v/c)
Eq. 18 — literal convolution in log-λ
fk(x)=∫g(s)Tk(x−s)ds=(Tk*g)(x)f_k(x) = \int g(s)\, T_k(x - s)\, ds = (T_k * g)(x)

That's what makes it a real, FFT-friendly convolution — and why the field calls this operation a "convolution" at all. kinextract_lite does not use this. A codebase-wide search confirms no log-rebinning anywhere: forward_model.shift_template applies the exact division below, then interpolates directly on the native (linear-Å) wavelength grid.

Eq. 19 — exact Doppler shift, as implemented
λrest=λobs1+v/c\lambda_{\text{rest}} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{obs}}}{1+v/c}

This is mathematically correct regardless of grid spacing — it doesn't rely on the log-space linear-shift approximation at all. The tradeoff is purely computational: per-bin interpolation instead of a handful of FFTs. As §6 shows, that cost is negligible (0.3% of a fit) at this problem size, so there's no real benefit to switching — though it would become one if a much larger template library (E-MILES's hundreds of SSPs, say) ever made template-stack construction a measurable fraction of total cost. One incidental upside of the current approach: it avoids log-rebinning the observed data, which itself introduces pixel-to-pixel noise correlation — a real confound this project's own noise-covariance work (§5) had to characterize and correct for.
